# Public Record Mining

Court filings, corporate registries, property records, and procurement databases are the backbone of accountability reporting — but they are also full of traps: similarly named entities, allegations mistaken for findings, and aggregator data detached from official sources. This skill reviews how records were found, verified, and used.

## Watch for
- Records pulled from aggregator sites without checking the underlying official source.
- Confusing similarly named entities (companies, people, courts) across jurisdictions.
- Using sealed, expunged, or restricted records as if they were public.
- Reporting a filing's allegation as an established fact.
- Bulk collection of personal data beyond what the story requires.
- No record of when and where each document was obtained.
- Overlooking the difference between a charge, a conviction, and a dismissal.
- Paying for records through channels that violate platform or agency rules.

## Best practices
- Trace every record back to the issuing office or official registry.
- Verify entity identity with at least two attributes (registration number, date, address).
- Note each document's legal status: allegation, adjudicated, sealed, expunged.
- Record retrieval date, source URL or office, and access method for every file.
- Collect the minimum personal data the story needs; mask what is not essential.
- Read the whole filing, not the excerpt that fits the narrative.
- Cross-reference registries across jurisdictions for the same entity.
- Respect terms of access; seek proper channels for restricted material.

## Quick checklist
- [ ] Every record was traced to an official source.
- [ ] Entity identities were verified with two attributes.
- [ ] The legal status of each filing is noted.
- [ ] Retrieval metadata is logged per document.
- [ ] Personal data collection is minimized and justified.
- [ ] Full documents were read, not just excerpts.
- [ ] Cross-jurisdiction checks were done for key entities.
- [ ] Access terms and restrictions were respected.
